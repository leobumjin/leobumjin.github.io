In 1912, Captain Robert Falcon Scott and his companions died during their return journey from the South Pole. Despite more than a century of knowledge about preventing scurvy, British polar expeditions continued to suffer from the disease. Scott's expedition physician had attributed scurvy to spoiled meat and questioned the effectiveness of citrus fruits. Consequently, Scott's polar party carried carefully preserved meat but no citrus fruits or juices. Although scurvy was not definitively established as the cause of Scott's death, the expedition illustrates how an incorrect understanding of causation can influence decisions with potentially tragic consequences.

The history of scurvy reveals how discovering a causal effect does not necessarily imply understanding its underlying mechanism. In 1747, James Lind demonstrated that citrus fruits could effectively treat scurvy. However, without knowing the role of vitamin C, physicians attributed the effect to the acidity of citrus fruits. This assumption encouraged the substitution of lemons with limes and the processing of their juice, which reduced its vitamin C content and weakened its preventive effect. Subsequent failures contributed to skepticism about citrus fruits and the emergence of alternative explanations, such as the belief that scurvy was caused by spoiled meat.

Fresh meat could make the spoiled-meat explanation appear more convincing. Raw or lightly cooked meat can retain small amounts of vitamin C, depending on the animal and tissue, whereas heating during preservation and prolonged storage can reduce that supply. A switch from preserved rations to fresh meat could therefore improve vitamin C intake, yet, without knowledge of the vitamin, recovery could be interpreted as evidence that avoiding spoiled meat was the cure. That interpretation could offer false reassurance: carefully preserved meat might seem sufficient as long as it was unspoiled, even if its preparation had left too little vitamin C. The intervention could work for a reason its advocates did not understand, reinforcing an explanation that would fail under different conditions.

This history reveals a subtle risk in causal reasoning. Moving from a general causal relationship to a more specific explanation is necessary for understanding and intervention, but greater specificity does not guarantee greater correctness. An unverified mechanism can introduce assumptions that undermine previously established knowledge. For example, observing that birds fly and possess wings might suggest that wings are sufficient for flight. Yet some birds have wings but cannot fly, because flight depends on multiple interacting factors. Similarly, even a correctly identified causal factor may not produce the same outcome when other contributing conditions change.

The challenge, therefore, is not merely to identify causal relationships but to understand their conditions of validity. Accurate causal knowledge can enable targeted interventions, allowing us to control relevant factors without unnecessarily altering others. However, when new evidence contradicts an existing explanation, we must distinguish between the failure of a proposed mechanism and the failure of the original causal effect.

The lesson of scurvy is ultimately one of epistemic caution: **causal explanations should become more specific only with sufficient justification, and new evidence should lead us to revise unsupported assumptions without unnecessarily abandoning well-established knowledge.** Reliable reasoning requires not only discovering causes, but also maintaining and revising our understanding of them as circumstances change.

## Historical context and sources

The historical sequence above is condensed. Citrus substitution and processing involved several decisions over time; acidity alone should not be read as a complete explanation of those decisions. The contribution of scurvy to the deaths of Scott's polar party remains uncertain.

- [Who was James Lind, and what exactly did he achieve?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3536506/) — historical discussion of Lind's experiment and the loss of vitamin C in his concentrated juice preparation.
- [100 Years Since Scott Reached the Pole: A Century of Learning About the Physiological Demands of Antarctica](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00031.2011) — discussion of expedition rations, uncertainty about scurvy, and contemporary distrust of citrus juice.
- [Scurvy, The Cambridge World History of Human Disease](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-world-history-of-human-disease/scurvy/16183A7447E831DBA04AD62940523342) — discussion of uncooked meat as a source of vitamin C and its sensitivity to heat.
- [A Short History of Nutritional Science: Part 2 (1885–1912)](https://jn.nutrition.org/content/133/4/975.full.pdf%2Bhtml) — historical accounts linking recovery on fresh meat to the mistaken tainted-food theory.
- [Vitamin C: Health Professional Fact Sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC-HealthProfessional/) — the role of vitamin C in scurvy and the effects of storage and cooking on vitamin C content.
